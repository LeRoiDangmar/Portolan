Four ways to draw overlapping network zones

Portolan draws networks as zones — enclosing areas around the bubbles that belong to them. That works until a container is attached to two networks at once, which in a real Swarm is not an edge case but the norm. Two areas must then intersect, and hull-based grouping either breaks or needs an explicit rule. This page exists so the rule can be chosen by looking, not by arguing. Four candidate rules, the same cluster in all four, no ranking and no recommendation.

⚠ Colour here is placeholder, not a palette proposal. The three hues exist only to tell three networks apart while you compare geometry, and the greys are deliberately inert. Nothing on this page is a brand or palette decision — that is a separate conversation, held after the zone rule is settled.

VARIANT 01

Intersecting hulls (Venn)

The rule: every network is one closed organic blob, and blobs are allowed to overlap. A container on two networks is placed in the lens where both blobs cover it — membership is read purely from where the bubble sits.

What it does well

Membership needs no convention and no legend: the lens is the answer to “which container is on both?”. One container appears exactly once, so counting by eye is correct and every edge leaves from the one true bubble. The two multi-homed services here (api, prometheus) are the first thing the eye lands on, which matches the question people open the tool with.

Where it breaks

Position is no longer free — it is dictated by set membership, so you cannot also use position for node, stack, or traffic direction. It only stays drawable while the networks form a chain: a container on three networks forces a genuine triple lens, and past four sets a faithful Euler diagram is not generally constructible in the plane at all. When it fails, it fails silently — the solver quietly places a bubble in a region that means something else.

What it costs at scale

Charming at 3 networks, dead by roughly 6. A typical Swarm host runs 8–12 overlay networks, which stacks 4–6 translucent layers on the same pixels and the hue stops resolving. Layout becomes constrained Euler-diagram embedding — expensive, non-deterministic between renders, and it needs a documented fallback. Edges get pushed across blob boundaries and are then read as membership by accident. Screenshots survive at this size only.

VARIANT 02

Disjoint hulls + echo bubble

The rule: blobs never touch. A multi-network container is drawn once in its primary zone and repeated as a lighter ghost in each other zone; a dotted “same container” tie joins the copies.

What it does well

Zone geometry becomes trivial and stable: disjoint regions can be gridded, sorted, filtered or collapsed, and adding a thirteenth network changes nothing about the first twelve. There is no impossible case — any membership count is expressible. Layout is cheap, deterministic, and identical between renders, so the picture is diffable.

Where it breaks

It lies about identity: api appears twice, so a headcount by eye is wrong unless the reader trusts the ghost convention. Non-network edges must then pick a copy — note the pgdata mount crossing the canvas from the primary api while the echo sits next to the volume. And “primary” is an invented notion: nothing in Swarm says frontend outranks backend, so an arbitrary choice moves the whole picture.

What it costs at scale

Bubble count grows with memberships, not containers: a cluster where most services sit on two networks nearly doubles its bubbles, and each extra membership adds another tie line across the full width. The ties are the first thing to become spaghetti, and they compete visually with the real edges — which the brief says are the product. Ghost styling must also survive greyscale printing and colour-blind viewers, since “real vs echo” cannot be carried by hue.

VARIANT 03

Hulls for exclusives + shared corridor

The rule: only containers on exactly one network sit inside that network's blob. Multi-network containers float in a neutral corridor between blobs and reach into each network through their labelled protrusions.

What it does well

It never has to draw an impossible region, so it is drawable for any membership count with no solver. The protrusions do real work here: the coloured stub into each blob is the membership statement, which is exactly Jules's signature idea carrying semantics rather than decoration. It also produces a free operational report — the corridor is the list of bridge containers, which is often the actual question behind “who talks to whom”.

Where it breaks

It exiles the interesting case: the multi-homed containers are the ones you opened the tool for, and they are the only ones with no home. The reader has to learn a rule (“middle means both”) that no diagram convention teaches. And a corridor only exists between adjacent zones — a container on frontend + monitoring has nowhere to sit unless zone ordering becomes a layout constraint or the corridor degenerates into one far-away “shared” pool.

What it costs at scale

The corridor becomes the busiest region of the canvas. Real bridge containers — ingress proxies, monitoring agents, log shippers — are numerous and heavily connected, so with 12 networks most bubbles and most edges end up in a single central column while the zone blobs sit sparse and decorative (already visible above at only 3 networks). Reach-connectors cross each other before the edges do. It does survive screenshots — nothing overlaps — but it eats horizontal space linearly with zones and vertical space linearly with bridge containers.

VARIANT 04

No hulls — tinted field / contour

The rule: a network is not an outline but a soft field of tint with contour lines. Fields multiply where they overlap, so a container on two networks sits on a blended colour — a cartographic reading rather than a set-theoretic one.

What it does well

No boundary means no impossible boundary: overlap is free, any membership count works, and no solver is involved. It hands position back to the graph — bubbles can be laid out for edge legibility, or by node or stack, which is the only variant here that lets the edges lead. Softness is honest about a cluster's real texture, and the organic language costs nothing extra.

Where it breaks

Membership becomes approximate. A field has no edge, so “is grafana in monitoring or merely near it?” has no answer at a glance, and a bubble drifting through a gradient is genuinely ambiguous. Blends are new colours — blue over amber reads as a fourth network, not as “both”. It is the weakest variant for colour-blind readers and it dies in greyscale, which matters because the export path for this tool is a screenshot.

What it costs at scale

Three fields blend acceptably; six turn the canvas to mud, and the number of distinguishable blends grows combinatorially rather than linearly, so the palette runs out long before the cluster does. To stay truthful it needs a per-bubble fallback (badge, hover-isolate, click-to-filter), which means the picture alone stops being the record and becomes an index into one — and a screenshot loses precisely the interaction that made it truthful. Cheapest to render, most expensive to trust.

No variant is marked as recommended, and the order is not a ranking. Each rule buys something and charges for it somewhere else — the trade is between reading membership from position (1, 3), from repetition (2), or from colour (4). The choice can also be split: one rule for the default overview, another for a focused single-network view. Written to be looked at, argued over, and then decided.
